What it actually feels like to design with an AI on your shoulder.

TB
Terrence Breschi · Personio, on the side

For the first six months, I thought I was just typing faster. Then I started noticing the smaller shifts — the way I'd start a sentence and let the Assistant finish it, the way I'd argue with a draft before I'd argue with myself.

Most people who write about designing with AI either pitch it like a productivity hack or panic about what it means for the craft. Neither of those describes what it actually feels like at the desk on a Tuesday.

What it feels like, mostly, is having someone sit slightly behind you. Not over your shoulder — that metaphor implies surveillance. More like a colleague who's quiet by default, but who has a useful thing to say if you turn and ask. The work is mine. The conversation is new.

“The work is mine. The conversation is new.”

Three places it actually helps.

The first is what I'd call scaffolding work — the kind of thing where you know the shape of the thing you want, but writing the first version is a slog. Empty states, error copy, the boring middle of a flow. The Assistant gets the scaffolding 70% right, which is enough to react to.

The second is rubber-ducking. I describe a problem out loud — in writing, at a keyboard — and the act of describing it well enough for the Assistant to respond usefully forces me to think about it. Half the time the answer isn't the Assistant's, it's mine, surfaced by the act of writing.

The third is harder to name. Something like refusing the obvious move. The Assistant produces a fluent default. I see that default and feel, often correctly, that the real answer is somewhere else.
